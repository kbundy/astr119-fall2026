> Why did you include a np.linspace variable for fracs?

● fracs is the step where the spec's main condition, equal amounts of paper per
  ring, becomes numbers the code can use.

  Equal steps go in paper, not radius. Five rings with equal amounts of paper
  means the boundaries sit at equal steps of fraction of paper remaining: 0,
  1/5, 2/5, 3/5, 4/5, 1. That's what np.linspace(0, 1, n + 1) produces: n + 1
  evenly spaced values from 0 to 1, with both ends included. Your
  product_visible then turns each fraction into the radius where that boundary
  sits.

  Why it's n + 1 and not n. Five rings need six circles: the core plus one outer
  edge per ring. It's the fence-post problem: n sections of fence need n + 1
  posts. That's also why np.diff(radii) later returns exactly n thicknesses.

  Why use linspace instead of typing the list. [0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1] would
  work for n = 5. linspace builds the list for any n, so visualize_danger(9)
  would also be correct.

  What it's not doing. It is not spacing the radii evenly. Suppose we had
  written radii = np.linspace(1/3, 1, n + 1) directly. You'd get rings of equal
  thickness, and those would hold unequal amounts of paper. The spec rules that
  out on line 5: the radius gaps must shrink as the circles get bigger.

  Your question to think about: the core has radius 1/3 and the edge has radius
  1 (in R_new units). If the rings had equal thickness instead, would the
  outermost ring hold more or less paper than the innermost one? Why? Think
  about how the area of a thin ring depends on its radius.
